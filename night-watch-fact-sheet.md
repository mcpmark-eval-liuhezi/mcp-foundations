## 2. Computed numbers (arithmetic)

Recorded dimensions: **379.5 cm (height) × 453.5 cm (width)**.

- Height in inches: 379.5 cm ÷ 2.54 cm per inch = **149.41 in** (≈ 149.4 in; ≈ 12 ft 5 in)
- Width in inches: 453.5 cm ÷ 2.54 cm per inch = **178.54 in** (≈ 178.5 in; ≈ 14 ft 10½ in)
- Surface area: 3.795 m × 4.535 m = **17.210325 m² ≈ 17.21 m²**
- Bonus: 17.210325 m² × 10.76391 ft²/m² = **≈ 185.25 ft²**

> **Cross-check observation:** Wikipedia gives 363 × 437 cm. The gap is exactly 16.5 cm on each axis (379.5 − 363 = 453.5 − 437 = 16.5) — i.e. 8.25 cm trimmed from each side on both height and width. Consistent with the 1715 cut-down: Wikipedia's 363 × 437 cm would then be the original size. Britannica, which agrees with the museum, gives 379.5 × 453.5 cm (12.4 × 14.9 ft).

## 3. The 18th-century story: painted 1642; moved to Amsterdam's town hall in 1715

**When it was painted:** The record dates it to **1642** (signed "Rembrandt f 1642"). The museum's provenance record says it was commissioned for the Great Hall (Groote Zaal) of the Kloveniersdoelen, the arquebusiers' civic-guard hall in Amsterdam — first documented there in 1653 ("Frans Benning Cock Capn. en Witt van Ruijtenburg Lut. geschildert van Rembrand an 1642"). Wikipedia adds it was commissioned around 1639 by Cocq and seventeen members of his company, possibly on the occasion of Marie de' Medici's 1638 visit; Rembrandt was paid 1,600 guilders (100 each); eighteen names appear on a shield painted circa 1715 in the centre of the composition, and the hired drummer was added for free.

**The 1715 move:** In **1715** the painting was moved from the Kloveniersdoelen to the **Amsterdam town hall** (Stadhuys) on Dam Square, and trimmed on **all four sides** — presumably to fit between two columns (Britannica adds: to fit through the doors of the town hall), a common practice before the 19th century. **That is 73 years after completion (1715 − 1642 = 73).** The town hall's war council resolved on 23 May 1715 to remove the great painting of Rembrandt from the hall of the doelen and place it in the war chamber (Kreygsraats Kamer) of the city hall.

**What the cut cost:** The trim removed the top of the arch, the balustrade, and the edge of the step, and took two figures off the left side of the composition (the left-hand section originally held three figures). The missing portions have never been found — though director Taco Dibbits has some hope the left side survived, since at the time Rembrandt paintings were already expensive.

**Notable additions from web sources not in the record:** the misnomer "The Night Watch" comes from a thick dark varnish (the simple title was first recorded in 1797, per Britannica; the record itself does not address the title's etymology); Gerrit Lundens' 17th-century copy (on loan from the National Gallery, London) shows the original composition; Rembrandt reportedly painted it in a lean-to in his garden because the canvas was too large for his studio; during WWII it was hidden in a coastal bunker and later the caves of St. Pietersburg near Maastricht (per Britannica).
